# 10. What NOT to track

The signals that look like data but are noise. Tracking them dilutes the metrics that matter.

## 1. Social media impressions on your own posts

Why people track it: feels like marketing data.

Why it is noise: impressions are not engagement; engagement is not conversion; conversion is not retention. The signal-to-noise is brutal. Most impressions come from algorithmic distribution that has nothing to do with your actual brand work.

What to track instead: replies and quote-tweets that name your brand specifically. Those are mentions.

## 2. Vanity follower counts

Why people track it: easy number that goes up.

Why it is noise: followers gained from one viral post are not your audience. They unfollow within weeks. The compound followers are the ones who came from your steady work, and you cannot tell them apart from the spike followers.

What to track instead: email subscribers (or RSS feed subscribers; whichever your audience uses).

## 3. Press release pickups

Why people track it: looks like "X publications covered us!"

Why it is noise: most "pickups" are PR Newswire and Bloomberg syndication republishing your release verbatim. Same content, different domain. Counts as one mention, not 47.

What to track instead: pickups where the journalist actually engaged with the content (added quotes, added context, fact-checked, picked their own angle).

## 4. Aggregator listings

Why people track it: feels like recognition.

Why it is noise: most aggregators auto-scrape and list everyone in a category. Being included means you exist on the public web. It does not mean anyone read it or took action.

What to track instead: aggregators that explicitly editorialize ("our top pick"). Those are mentions worth tracking.

## 5. Generic backlinks from low-authority sites

Why people track it: SEO tools count them.

Why it is noise: links from PBN-style sites and link farms damage you, not help. Counting them gives the wrong impression of progress.

What to track instead: backlinks from sites with real editorial standards (your own niche's recognized publications, .edu, .gov, established blogs).

## 6. Pure positive sentiment when you do not engage

Why people track it: feels like a popularity score.

Why it is noise: a stream of vague positive mentions ("love this tool!") that never convert to anything is comfortable but not useful. The hard mentions (criticism, comparison, debate) are higher signal.

What to track instead: mentions that engage substantively, positive or negative.

## 7. Daily mention spikes

Why people track it: alerts notify you in real time.

Why it is noise: most spikes are random. A piece you wrote 2 years ago resurfaces in someone's Slack and gets shared. You spend a day chasing the spike and the underlying baseline does not change.

What to track instead: 30-day rolling averages. If the rolling average is moving, that is signal. If only one day spiked, that is variance.

## 8. Mentions in languages you do not work in

Why people track it: looks international.

Why it is noise: if your product is English-only, a mention in Vietnamese is interesting trivia but not actionable. You cannot follow up, cannot build a relationship, cannot serve the audience.

What to track instead: mentions in your active languages. Note mentions in other languages quarterly as a "should we translate?" signal, not as ongoing data.

## 9. Bot mentions and spam

Why people track it: alerts include them.

Why it is noise: an obvious spam comment naming your brand does not signal anything.

What to track instead: filter out from the moment you log. Add a filter rule to your alerts: exclude common spam patterns (`-casino -viagra -seo`).

## 10. Mentions from your own employees and affiliates

Why people track it: looks like word-of-mouth.

Why it is noise: paid affiliates posting your brand link is paid marketing. Employees posting are employee advocacy. Both are valid but should be tracked separately, not mixed with organic mentions.

What to track instead: an "internal channels" tab in the spreadsheet for these. Aggregate metric only.

## 11. Brand mentions with no context

Example: a tweet that just says "yourbrand.com" with no commentary.

Why it is noise: someone may have shared the link, may have been describing a typo, may have been quoting someone else. Without context, you cannot tell what it means.

What to track instead: only mentions where someone said something about you, not just dropped a URL.

## 12. SEO position changes for non-branded keywords

Why people track it: SEO tools show daily changes.

Why it is noise: position 7 to position 9 for a long-tail keyword nobody searches is meaningless. Position changes are noisy and most movement is statistical noise, not real signal.

What to track instead: branded queries (do people search for your brand by name?) and the AI engine direct test.

## 13. Your competitors' mention volumes

Why people track it: feels strategic.

Why it is noise: their volume tells you nothing about whether their volume is converting for them or whether they would trade with you. Comparing apples to oranges to grapes.

What to track instead: only competitor mentions that include comparison to you ("X vs Competitor"). Those are mentions in your audience's decision process.

## 14. Engagement on platforms you do not actively work

Why people track it: dashboards show every platform.

Why it is noise: if you don't post on Threads, a mention on Threads is data but not actionable. You cannot scale a presence on a platform you don't engage on.

What to track instead: pick the 2 or 3 platforms you actually work on; track those deeply. Note others quarterly as a "is this growing without us?" question.

## 15. Mentions of generic terms you happen to own

Example: if you named your brand "Coffee", every mention of coffee triggers an alert.

Why it is noise: the false-positive rate is 99 percent. You spend more time filtering than learning.

What to track instead: refine the query. Add context terms that disambiguate. If you cannot disambiguate, the brand name is the actual problem; consider rebrand.

## The general principle

Track what you can act on. A signal that does not change behavior is not a signal; it is decoration.

After every monthly review, ask: did I do anything different because of the data I collected? If no, you are tracking too much.

Cut the tracker columns that have no action attached. Cut the alerts that produce only noise. Cut the metrics that look impressive in slides but do not affect decisions.

The tracker should be small enough to be used. A tracker that is too big to maintain is worse than no tracker.
