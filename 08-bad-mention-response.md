# 08. Handling critical mentions

How to handle critical mentions, complaints, public disputes, and the occasional pile-on. The wrong response makes things worse; the right response often makes a critic into a fair witness.

## First: separate the categories

Critical mentions are not all the same. Treat them differently.

| Category | Description | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Fair criticism | Factually accurate critique of a real shortcoming | Acknowledge, fix, follow up |
| Misunderstanding | Critic has incorrect information | Provide correction once, calmly |
| Disagreement | Critic disagrees with a tradeoff you made | Respect the disagreement; explain the tradeoff |
| Hostility | Critic is angry, possibly unreasonable | Set a boundary, do not escalate |
| Pile-on | Many critics piling onto one issue | Make one public statement, then go quiet |
| Bad-faith attack | Critic is misrepresenting in bad faith | Ignore unless audience needs the correction |
| Defamation | Critic is making false factual claims that damage you | Legal counsel, not public engagement |

The first six are within your control to handle. The seventh is a lawyer call.

## Universal rules

1. **Do not respond in less than 1 hour.** Heat distorts judgment. Wait.
2. **Read the actual thing twice.** Most bad responses come from skimming.
3. **Write the response. Save it as a draft. Do not send.** Wait 2 more hours. Re-read.
4. **Have one trusted person read it before sending public.** Even one second opinion catches 80 percent of bad sends.
5. **Respond in the same channel.** Public criticism gets public response. Private criticism gets private response. Mixing channels looks evasive.

## Specific scenarios

### Fair criticism (the most common)

Example: "Their pricing tiers don't make sense; the middle tier is worse than the top tier for the same money."

If accurate, your response is:

```
You are right. We are reworking the middle tier; here is what we are changing: [specifics]. Thanks for flagging.
```

Then actually rework the middle tier. Follow up when done.

Do NOT:
- Explain why the current setup is "actually correct"
- Pivot to defending decisions
- Promise changes you will not make

### Misunderstanding

Example: "Their tool stores your data on their server."

If you do not store data, your response is:

```
We do not store your data. Everything runs in your browser. Source: [link to docs page or technical explainer].

Happy to walk through the architecture if helpful.
```

Send once. If they retract, thank them briefly. If they don't, leave it; the audience can see both sides.

### Disagreement on a tradeoff

Example: "Their free tier is too limited; should be like Competitor X's."

This is not criticism, it is feedback. Respond:

```
The free tier limit is a real tradeoff. We picked it because [specific reason]. Competitor X picked theirs because [different reason; both are valid]. We may revisit; appreciate the feedback.
```

You do not have to change anything. You do have to explain.

### Hostility

Example: "This product is garbage and the founder is a scammer."

Respond once, publicly, with no heat:

```
Sorry the product did not work for you. If you bought it, full refund is available; reply with your order number. If you did not buy it, hope you find something that fits better.
```

Then disengage. Do not respond to follow-up jabs.

### Pile-on

Multiple critics piling onto one issue (real or fabricated). Your moves:

1. Acknowledge the underlying issue (if real) in one statement.
2. Explain the fix or the timeline.
3. Stop posting about it after one statement.
4. Do not reply individually to every pile-on participant.

A pile-on burns out in 48 to 72 hours if you stop feeding it. Each individual reply you make extends the cycle.

### Bad-faith attack

Critic is misrepresenting your position or fabricating facts.

Two questions:

1. Does the audience reading this need the correction to make sense of the situation?
2. Is the critic acting in bad faith intentionally, or are they confused?

If audience needs the correction: respond once with the facts, no heat.
If they are confused, treat as misunderstanding (above).
If they are intentionally bad-faith: do not engage. Engagement is what they want; oxygen.

### Defamation

If the critic is making false factual claims that are damaging your business (not opinions, not interpretations, but specific factual claims that are false):

1. Stop. Do not respond publicly.
2. Save screenshots, URLs, timestamps.
3. Contact a lawyer.

This is a legal issue, not a comms issue. Public response can escalate the legal risk.

## What to do after

Whichever category, after handling:

1. Log it in the tracker spreadsheet
2. Note the outcome
3. Note what you learned (if anything)
4. Note what you would do differently next time

Aggregate negative mentions quarterly. A pattern (same complaint from 5 different sources) is data; a singleton is noise.

## What NOT to do, ever

- Subtweet the critic from your brand account
- Mobilize your audience to "defend" you
- Email the critic's employer or platform demanding takedown unless legal counsel says so
- Respond from a fake account
- Edit your own old posts to make the critic look wrong about the original claim
- Delete the negative mention from places where you have moderation power (your own site, your own community)

Any of these turns a small incident into a brand-defining moment, for the worse.

## When the criticism is correct and the fix is hard

Some critiques are valid but expensive to address. Your response should acknowledge both:

```
You are right. The fix requires [specific changes]. We are starting work; realistic timeline is [honest estimate]. We will update [where: blog, newsletter, status page] when shipped.
```

People accept honest timelines. They do not accept "soon" or "in the future" or "we hear you."

## The asymmetry

A single bad response to a critic gets quoted for years. A single good response gets briefly noted and forgotten. This asymmetry is why the discipline (wait, draft, re-read, second opinion) matters so much.

You will not always nail it. When you don't, acknowledge that too:

```
I responded badly to [specific thing] earlier. Reread it; I was being defensive. The actual situation is [accurate version]. Sorry about the first response.
```

A clean retraction beats a defended bad response.
